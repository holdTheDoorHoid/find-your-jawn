# Evidence Base: Quiz, Ranking, Stretch Picks, and Follow-Through

Scouting report for the Philadelphia community/volunteering directory and matching quiz. Every rule below is backed by a verifiable, cited source. Where a claim could not be independently confirmed this session, it is marked "weak / unverified" rather than presented as settled.

## Design Rules

1. Build the quiz around the six Volunteer Functions Inventory (VFI) motive categories (values, understanding, social, career, protective, enhancement) instead of generic interest tags, and score candidate matches by how well they serve a person's own top motive.
   Evidence: Clary, Snyder, Ridge, Copeland, Stukas, Haugen, and Miene (1998), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 74(6), 1516 to 1530. Across six studies, volunteers whose activities matched their primary motive reported higher satisfaction and stronger intentions to continue than those in motive-mismatched roles.
   Confidence: strong

2. Alongside motive-matching, score each candidate opportunity for how much autonomy, skill-building (competence), and belonging (relatedness) it is likely to offer, since these three needs predict engagement and retention independent of which VFI motive is in play.
   Evidence: Zheng, Yao, Zhang, Li, and Xing (2021), International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 18, article 13229. Basic psychological need satisfaction (autonomy, competence, relatedness) predicted sustained volunteering among college students in a mixed-methods study.
   Confidence: moderate

3. Cap the default results screen at roughly five to nine ranked matches rather than a long scrollable list, especially for a first-time or uncertain user.
   Evidence: Iyengar and Lepper (2000), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79(6), 995 to 1006, the original choice-overload "jam study"; a widely cited secondary report of that study found 30 percent of shoppers who sampled from a 6-item display purchased versus 3 percent from a 24-item display. Chernev, Böckenholt, and Goodman (2015), Journal of Consumer Psychology, 25(2), 333 to 358, a meta-analysis of 99 effect sizes, confirmed the underlying overload effect is real but conditional.
   Confidence: strong

4. Scale the number of options shown to how certain the user's preferences are: show fewer, more tightly ranked matches early (when preferences are vague) and allow a larger browsable set once the user has given specific, narrow signals.
   Evidence: Chernev, Böckenholt, and Goodman (2015), as above. Choice overload is worsened by high preference uncertainty, high decision-task difficulty, complex option sets, and an effort-minimizing goal, all of which describe a first-time quiz taker; it is much weaker once preferences are clear.
   Confidence: moderate

5. Limit "stretch" picks to one or two per result set, and calibrate them to be moderately novel relative to the user's stated interests rather than maximally different, since both familiarity and novelty independently drive appeal and too much of either suppresses it.
   Evidence: Hekkert, Snelders, and van Wieringen (2003), British Journal of Psychology, 94, 111 to 124, the "most advanced yet acceptable" (MAYA) study. Typicality and novelty both predicted aesthetic preference in product design, and preference dropped once novelty passed a moderate threshold.
   Confidence: moderate (industrial-design research, applied here by analogy to recommendation design)

6. Frame at least one stretch pick as a shared activity to try with another person from the quiz or a friend, not a solo assignment, since jointly doing something novel and mildly challenging reliably increases relationship quality and motivation, even in brief doses.
   Evidence: Aron, Norman, Aron, McKenna, and Heyman (2000), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 273 to 284. Couples assigned novel and arousing shared activities reported higher relationship quality than couples assigned pleasant but familiar activities.
   Confidence: moderate (originally relationship-dyad evidence, generalized here to acquaintance and community contexts)

7. Attach a short, specific "why this fits you" explanation to every match, tying it to the user's own quiz answers rather than a generic "recommended for you" label.
   Evidence: Herlocker, Konstan, and Riedl (2000), Proceedings of the 2000 ACM Conference on Computer Supported Cooperative Work, 241 to 250. Explanations increased users' acceptance of collaborative-filtering recommendations in controlled tests.
   Confidence: strong

8. For stretch picks specifically, say outright that the pick is a deliberate stretch and explain why it might still fit, rather than presenting it identically to a close match, since users who understand and relate to why something novel was suggested are more likely to follow up on it.
   Evidence: Smets, Vannieuwenhuyze, and Ballon (2021), Journal of the Association for Information Science and Technology, 73(1). In a survey of 1,641 citizens using urban recommender systems, the relevance and novelty of a recommendation strongly predicted experienced serendipity, and experienced serendipity increased the odds of the person following up.
   Confidence: moderate

9. End every match with a concrete next step, a specific date, time, location, or named contact to reach out to, rather than stopping at an organization name and description.
   Evidence: Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006), Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69 to 119, a meta-analysis of 94 independent tests with over 8,000 participants. Forming a specific "if-then" plan (an implementation intention) had a medium-to-large effect on whether a goal was actually acted on (d = 0.65).
   Confidence: strong

10. Offer to turn a match into a commitment device at the moment of highest motivation, for example a calendar hold, a text reminder, or an emailed plan the user can forward to a friend, instead of only a link out to the organization's own site.
    Evidence: Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006), as above. The mechanism behind the implementation-intention effect is a concrete, pre-committed plan; a calendar hold or reminder operationalizes that plan at the point of decision.
    Confidence: moderate

11. Build a "bring someone" option into every match and make personal invitation a first-class feature, not an afterthought, since not being personally asked is one of the most common reasons people who are open to joining never do, and weak-tie connections are disproportionately how people learn about opportunities outside their existing circle.
    Evidence: National Council for Voluntary Organisations, Time Well Spent 2023 (UK national volunteering survey): "never been asked" (16 percent) and "never thought about it" (19 percent) were commonly cited reasons for not volunteering. Granovetter (1973), American Journal of Sociology, 78(6), 1360 to 1380, "The Strength of Weak Ties," established that acquaintances (weak ties), not close friends, are disproportionately the channel through which people learn about opportunities.
    Confidence: moderate to strong

12. Deliberately surface some "bridging" matches, ones that connect the user to people from a different neighborhood, age group, or background, alongside "bonding" matches among people like them, and label which kind each match is, since the two produce different and complementary social-capital benefits that users rarely select for themselves.
    Evidence: Putnam (2000), Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community, Simon and Schuster, the foundational distinction between bridging social capital (linking across groups) and bonding social capital (reinforcing ties within a group); Granovetter (1973), as above, on weak ties as the primary source of non-redundant information.
    Confidence: moderate (foundational sociological theory, not an experimental test of the labeling intervention itself)

13. Weight recurring, place-based, low-cost-of-entry groups (coffee-shop meetups, library programs, recreation-center drop-ins) somewhat higher than one-off signup events in the ranking, since repeated informal contact in a neutral, accessible venue is what produces durable community ties.
    Evidence: Oldenburg (1989), The Great Good Place, Paragon House, the original description of "third places": neutral ground, low barrier to entry, regulars, and conversation as the main activity, as a distinct category of community infrastructure separate from home and work.
    Confidence: moderate (descriptive sociological theory, not a randomized test)

14. Give every listed opportunity a short, plain-language "first visit" section that states directly that feeling unsure the first time is normal, describes what to expect, and explains how newcomers are welcomed, rather than leaving first-visit anxiety unaddressed.
    Evidence: Walton and Cohen (2007), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 92(1), 82 to 96, which introduced "belonging uncertainty," the doubt that one will be accepted in a new setting. Walton and Cohen (2011), Science, 331(6023), 1447 to 1451, showed that a single one-hour exercise normalizing early belonging worries produced measurable academic and health gains for students years later, with the largest gains for students from negatively stereotyped or underrepresented groups.
    Confidence: strong

15. Design the matching flow and the organization listings to blend "mobilizing" opportunities (easy, one-off, low-commitment entry points) with "organizing" opportunities (ones that build the volunteer's own skills, leadership, and relationships over time), and use early mobilizing-style matches as an explicit on-ramp toward deeper roles rather than ranking purely on single-session fit.
    Evidence: Hahrie Han (2014), How Organizations Develop Activists: Civic Associations and Leadership in the 21st Century, Oxford University Press. Fieldwork across civic associations found the highest-engagement organizations deliberately combine mobilizing (which builds breadth) with organizing (which builds depth), using mobilizing contacts as entry points cultivated into deeper involvement.
    Confidence: moderate

16. Treat reducing loneliness and isolation as a named, explicit goal of the directory, not an incidental side effect of volunteering, and route users who signal isolation or newcomer status toward connection-first opportunities, not just task-based ones.
    Evidence: U.S. Surgeon General (2023), Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation: The U.S. Surgeon General's Advisory on the Healing Effects of Social Connection and Community. The advisory reports that about half of U.S. adults experience loneliness and that lacking social connection is associated with a mortality risk comparable to smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day, based on the advisory's review of the pooled epidemiological literature.
    Confidence: strong (for the scale of the problem as characterized by the advisory itself; the smoking comparison is the advisory's own framing of earlier meta-analytic mortality research, not a new primary study)

17. Build a brief, low-friction follow-up nudge after a match is made (for example, a message a few days later asking whether the person followed through, with an easy one-tap re-match if not), since a personal, specific prompt is what converts intention into action and a second prompt helps sustain it.
    Evidence: Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006), as above, on the mechanism of implementation intentions; National Council for Voluntary Organisations, Time Well Spent 2023, on "never been asked" as a leading reported barrier, implying that a direct, personal prompt closes a gap that passive information does not.
    Confidence: moderate

18. Sequence the user's path so a small, easy, no-commitment first ask (attend one event, join one group chat) is offered before any ask for an ongoing or recurring role, using the completed small ask to support a later request for deeper commitment.
    Evidence: Freedman and Fraser (1966), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 4(2), 195 to 202, "Compliance without pressure: The foot-in-the-door technique," the original demonstration that agreeing to a small initial request increases later compliance with a larger request. Consistent with Hahrie Han's (2014) mobilizing-to-organizing ladder described above.
    Confidence: moderate

19. Make practical access barriers (cost, schedule and shift-friendliness, physical accessibility, languages offered, transit access, and whether a background check is required) structured, filterable fields on every listing rather than information buried in free-text descriptions.
    Evidence: National Council for Voluntary Organisations, Time Well Spent 2023: among people who looked into volunteering but did not proceed, the leading reasons were that it required more time than they could commit (21 percent), there was nothing flexible enough (14 percent), and out-of-pocket cost concerns such as transport rose from 5 percent (2019) to 14 percent (2023).
    Confidence: moderate (UK national survey; used here as general evidence on the shape of access barriers, not as a Philadelphia-specific figure)

20. For any listing that involves routine contact with children, show Pennsylvania's Act 153 clearance requirement up front in plain language (a Pennsylvania State Police criminal history check, a Child Abuse History Clearance from the PA Department of Human Services, and an FBI fingerprint-based clearance, with the FBI step waived for unpaid volunteers who have lived in Pennsylvania continuously for the past 10 years), with a direct link to start the process, because this is a mandatory, non-obvious, multi-week barrier specific to Pennsylvania that can silently kill sign-ups for youth-facing roles if it surfaces too late in the flow.
    Evidence: Act 153 of 2014 (amending the PA Child Protective Services Law); consistent institutional summaries from Carnegie Mellon University, Duquesne University, Justifacts, and FIRST Inspires all describe the same three clearances, the 10-year-residency FBI exemption for unpaid volunteers, and a 60-month renewal cycle.
    Confidence: strong (statutory requirement; this is a compliance fact rather than a behavioral-science finding, but it is directly load-bearing for the product)

21. Offer the quiz and the highest-traffic listings in Philadelphia's most common non-English languages, and let organizations flag which languages their staff or volunteers can support, treating language access as a distinct barrier from motivation that better matching alone cannot fix.
    Evidence: general inclusive-design and language-access practice. This session could not independently verify a current, Philadelphia-specific dataset of resident languages or a direct study linking language access to volunteering uptake; treat this rule as directionally sound but confirm against city or Census American Community Survey language data before finalizing which languages to prioritize.
    Confidence: weak / unverified this session, flagged for follow-up research

22. Maintain a distinctly labeled, vetted sub-list of organizations that explicitly and verifiably welcome people with criminal records, and route users who disclose a record toward that sub-list, since background-check and clearance requirements (including Act 153 above) can otherwise silently filter this population out of nearly every listing without anyone noticing.
    Evidence: inferred from the Act 153 clearance requirements above and the general pattern that background-check requirements function as an access barrier; this session did not locate a dedicated Pennsylvania-specific study quantifying how many returning citizens are deterred from volunteering by clearance requirements, so treat the underlying mechanism as plausible but unquantified.
    Confidence: weak / unverified this session, flagged for follow-up research

23. Keep the quiz itself short enough to complete in a few minutes and avoid asking for more information than the matching engine will actually use, since every additional required field is itself a small instance of the same cost-benefit calculation that drives choice overload and abandonment in the results screen.
    Evidence: extension of Chernev, Böckenholt, and Goodman (2015), as above, on decision-task difficulty and effort-minimizing goals as conditions that worsen overload; applied here to the input side of the flow rather than only the output side.
    Confidence: moderate (reasoned extension of a verified finding, not a direct test of quiz length itself)

## Philadelphia and Pennsylvania Statistics

Source for all figures below: AmeriCorps and the U.S. Census Bureau, Current Population Survey Civic Engagement and Volunteering Supplement (CEV), as published on americorps.gov.

- Pennsylvania formal volunteering rate (volunteered through an organization in the past 12 months): 34.2 percent in 2017, 35.8 percent in 2019, 25.6 percent in 2021, and 31.0 percent in 2023. Source: AmeriCorps, State Trends Over Time, https://americorps.gov/about/our-impact/volunteering-civic-life/state-trends
- Philadelphia metro area formal volunteering rate: 28.7 percent in 2021 and 32.9 percent in 2023. Source: AmeriCorps, Philadelphia Metro Area, https://americorps.gov/about/our-impact/volunteering-civic-life/philadelphia-metro-area
- Philadelphia metro area informal helping rate (helped a neighbor directly, outside any organization): 57.8 percent in 2021, second only to Boston's 57.9 percent among large metros that year, and 64.9 percent in 2023. Source: same AmeriCorps Philadelphia Metro Area page, corroborated by U.S. Census Bureau, "At Height of Pandemic, More Than Half of People Age 16 and Over Helped Neighbors, 23% Formally Volunteered," https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2023/01/volunteering-and-civic-life-in-america.html
- Design implication: roughly two in three Philadelphians already informally help neighbors, nearly double the share who volunteer through a formal organization. The directory's practical job is as much about converting that existing informal-helping instinct into a first formal connection as it is about finding people who are not already inclined to help at all.
- This session could not independently verify a Philadelphia- or Pennsylvania-specific breakdown of the top stated reasons for not volunteering (the national AmeriCorps research-summary PDF returned an access error when fetched directly). The barrier figures cited in the Design Rules section above (not being asked, cost, inflexibility) come from a UK national survey (NCVO, Time Well Spent 2023) used as general evidence of the shape of these barriers, not as a Philadelphia or Pennsylvania statistic. Flagged for follow-up with a direct pull from data.americorps.gov's CEV dataset if a precise local breakdown is needed.

### Pennsylvania Act 153 volunteer clearances

Pennsylvania Act 153 of 2014 requires anyone, paid or volunteer, who has routine interaction with children to obtain three clearances before starting:

1. A Pennsylvania State Police criminal history report.
2. A Pennsylvania Child Abuse History Clearance from the Department of Human Services.
3. A fingerprint-based FBI federal criminal history check, submitted through the Pennsylvania State Police or an authorized vendor.

Unpaid volunteers who have been continuous Pennsylvania residents for the past 10 years may sign a sworn waiver in place of the FBI fingerprint clearance. Clearances must be renewed every 60 months. Sources: institutional summaries from Carnegie Mellon University (https://www.cmu.edu/hr/resources/child-protection/act-153/index.html), Duquesne University (https://www.duq.edu/work-at-du/pa-act-153.php), Justifacts (https://www.justifacts.com/resources/pa-act-153-clearance), and FIRST Inspires' volunteer guide (https://www.firstinspires.org/hubfs/web/about/policy/PA-Act-153-Guide-How-to-complete-clearances.pdf), all consistent on these requirements.
